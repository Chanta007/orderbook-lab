# Research result

**Status: Partial**

Defensible numbers in a Rust market-data system come from four disciplines: timestamp with a monotonic clock read once per message, record into a coordinated-omission-corrected histogram and report tails rather than means, score execution against an explicitly chosen reference price and sign convention, and treat backpressure as a budget with documented thresholds and alerts. The platform specifics differ — Apple silicon has no user-readable x86-style TSC and relies on the ARM generic-timer counter, Linux x86-64 offers CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW or a serialization-sensitive rdtsc, and macOS pairs mach_absolute_time with mach_continuous_time when elapsed time must include sleep. The numbers are only as defensible as the treatment of clock skew, counter synchronization, sampling coverage, and the reference price chosen for scoring.

### Clock sources for nanosecond timestamping

macOS offers two monotonic tick-unit clocks that differ only in sleep behavior: mach_absolute_time does not advance while the system is asleep, while mach_continuous_time (macOS 10.12+) does [S1]. clock_gettime has been available since 10.12, and its variants map onto that pair: CLOCK_UPTIME_RAW returns exactly mach_absolute_time after the mach_timebase conversion, CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW is unaffected by frequency or time adjustments, and clock_gettime_nsec_np returns 64-bit nanoseconds directly [S2]. On Apple silicon there is no x86-style TSC: mach_absolute_time is a userspace EL0 read of the ARM generic-timer virtual counter CNTVCT_EL0 plus a commpage offset, with CNTVCTSS_EL0 and ACNTVCT_EL0 variants and a kernel-trap fallback, while the x86_64 build of the same function uses rdtsc [S3]. In the kernel, mach_continuous_time returns the raw hardware clock where a continuous one exists and otherwise mach_absolute_time plus the accumulated sleep offset (mach_absolutetime_asleep), which the wake path accumulates and republishes to the commpage [S4].

On Linux, clock_gettime — often served from the vDSO — provides CLOCK_MONOTONIC, which is affected by frequency adjustments but never jumps discontinuously and does not count suspend time, and CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW (since Linux 2.6.28), which reads raw hardware-based time not subject to frequency adjustments; monotonic variants never go backwards, though consecutive calls may return identical values [S5]. On x86-64, rdtsc reads a 64-bit TSC [S6].

### Latency histograms, tail quantiles, and coordinated omission

HdrHistogram records and analyzes sampled value counts across a configurable integer value range with configurable precision in significant digits, and keeps a fixed memory footprint with no allocation during recording or iteration, which is what makes it usable on a latency-sensitive path [S7]. The Rust port offers three constructors: ::new (auto-resizing, precision only), ::new_with_max (bounded, auto-resize disabled, with record returning an error on overflow), and ::new_with_bounds(low, high, sigfig) — for example, Histogram::<u64>::new_with_bounds(1, 60 * 60 * 1000, 2) covers roughly 1 ms to 1 hour at about 1% precision [S8]. Read tails with value_at_quantile(q), which returns the value that the given fraction of recorded entries are smaller than or equal to (for q > 0.0); value_at_percentile is that value multiplied by 100.0; and value_at_quantiles/value_at_percentiles compute multiple quantiles in a single pass over the counts array, which is faster than N separate calls [S9].

Two reporting choices distort the picture even when the clock is perfect. The mean — and standard deviation, or a lone 90th percentile — misleads because latency is heavy-tailed rather than normally distributed: reporting only the mean or the 90th percentile is a common mistake, and the average-and-standard-deviation "wishful thinking" assumes a normal distribution, whereas the real distribution put a 99.999% value roughly 184 standard deviations from the mean [S12]. Coordinated omission is the second: a stall contributes almost no samples, so its cost disappears from the percentiles, and correction is available either at recording time through record_correct(value, interval)/record_n_correct, which auto-generate a series of decreasingly smaller records down to interval whenever a value exceeds the expected sampling interval, or after the fact through clone_correct(interval)/set_to_corrected/add_correct — the two approaches being mutually exclusive on a given dataset [S10]. The canonical illustration: sampling every 10 ms through 100 s of perfect 1 ms responses and then a 100 s pause recorded as a single sample, the raw histogram reports ~99.99% of results at or below 1 ms, while the corrected histogram (expected interval 10 ms) reports only ~50%, with the rest spread between 10 ms and 100 s [S11].

### Scoring execution against a reference price

The SEC Rule 605 effective spread (17 CFR 242.600(b)(8)) is the share-weighted average of per-execution effective spreads: double the difference between the execution price and the NBBO midpoint at order receipt for a buy, and double the difference between that midpoint and the execution price for a sell; midpoint-or-better limit orders use the midpoint when the order first becomes executable, and the convention is signed, with a positive value being a cost to the order sender [S13]. Realized spread (17 CFR 242.600(b)(13)) is built the same way but against the NBBO midpoint at a specified interval after execution — falling back to the final regular-trading-hours midpoint if the interval runs past RTH — so its reference is post-trade rather than at receipt [S14]. MiFID II RTS 27 (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/575) requires venues to publish an average effective spread (Annex Table 8) and the best bid and offer or suitable reference price at time of execution (Annex Table 3), and its implementation formula — Sum(2 × |Transaction Price − Midpoint|) / Number of Transactions — is unsigned, an absolute deviation rather than the signed SEC convention [S15].

Markout is the post-trade comparison: Markout(h) = side × [midpoint(t+h) − execution price], with side +1 for a buy and −1 for a sell, positive being favorable to the assigned side and reversing sign between the taker and the liquidity provider; a mid-to-mid variant compares the midpoint at execution with the later midpoint to isolate signed price impact, and slippage by contrast compares an execution with a pre-trade or contemporaneous benchmark [S16]. Implementation shortfall (Perold 1988) is the difference between the return of a paper portfolio — the full intended quantity transacted instantly at the decision price — and the actual return after real fills and frictions: IS = Paper Return − Actual Return, the standard total-cost-of-trade measure, decomposing into delay cost, execution (market-impact) cost, and opportunity cost on unfilled shares plus explicit fees; Perold's formula is IS = Σs_j·(P_avg − P_d) + (S − Σs_j)·(P_n − P_d) + fees, and an arrival-price variant substitutes the mid-quote or last price at order arrival when the decision price is unknown [S17].

### Quantifying and alerting on backpressure

Kafka surfaces consumer backpressure as the JMX metric records-lag-max — the maximum record lag for any assigned partition in the metrics window — and a rising value is the primary indication that the consumer group is not keeping up [S18]. At the broker, request-queue depth is bounded by a high-water mark: queued.max.requests defaults to 500, above which the network threads block and stop reading, while the byte-based queued.max.request.bytes defaults to −1, meaning disabled [S19]. JetStream defines lag as the stream's last_seq minus the consumer's delivered stream sequence, reported directly for pull consumers as num_pending, with num_ack_pending separately counting in-flight, delivered-but-unacked messages [S20]. Alert thresholds are expressed as percentages of configured limits — ack-pending buildup warns when num_ack_pending exceeds 90% (default) of max_ack_pending, with 80%+ the danger zone against a default max_ack_pending of 1,000 — or as operator-defined thresholds, as with the unprocessed-critical check on num_pending [S21]. Clients bound the pending buffer by message and byte limits (500,000 messages and 64 MB by default in the Go client, with Pending()/PendingLimits() exposing usage), surface overflow as the slow-consumer signal, and expose a running drop count through Dropped() [S22]. A push-based exchange feed cannot absorb backpressure at all: a filled TCP receive buffer closes the window, sustained backpressure causes the exchange to terminate the session with no prior notification — the first indication is an onError callback — so the handler must choose an explicit queue-full policy of dropping, circuit-breaking, or alerting and disconnecting [S23].

### Pitfalls that silently invalidate latency numbers

The TSC is the main silent invalidator: it is CPU-local and may be unsynchronized across cores, sockets, and NUMA nodes, may stop in C-states, and may change rate with P-states, which the constant_tsc and nonstop_tsc feature bits model; precise measurement requires a serializing instruction such as CPUID or an MSR read, and virtualization adds a guest TSC offset [S6]. For one-way latency across sources, CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW carries an explicit caveat that it should not be compared with other system time sources [S2]. Findings on same-thread measurement effects, page faults, first-touch and mlock, timer coalescing, and container-level jitter were not part of the evidence reviewed.
